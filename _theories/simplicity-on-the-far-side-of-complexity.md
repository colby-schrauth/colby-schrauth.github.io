Anyone can be simple at the start. Being simple at the end, after you've lived the mess, is what proves you understand.

## The idea

Understanding anything hard moves through three stages. First, it looks simple, because you don't know enough to see the problems. Then it gets messy: you're deep in the details, and every answer starts with "it depends." Finally, if you stay with it, it gets simple again, but now the simplicity is compressed from everything you learned.

{% include curve.html id="simplicity" %}

The first and last stages sound alike. "Customers churn because the product is hard to use" can be a guess or a conclusion. The difference shows the moment someone asks a follow-up: earned simplicity has depth behind it; naive simplicity has nothing.

## Where it comes from

The idea is old, and usually quoted as "I would not give a fig for the simplicity this side of complexity, but I would give my life for the simplicity on the other side of complexity." It's most often attributed to Oliver Wendell Holmes, though the exact wording and source are disputed.

## Why it matters

Compression is the proof. Being able to say something hard in one sentence is evidence you've done the work, because you can only cut what you understand. That's why the clearest people in a room are rarely the newest, and why "it's complicated" is usually a sign someone is still in the middle.

## A framework inside it

Clarity is the foundation of [Executive Presence]({{ '/frameworks/executive-presence.html' | relative_url }}): people trust a simple answer when they can see the mess behind it.

## Where it breaks

- **You can't tell the two simples apart at a glance.** Ask one follow-up question before trusting either.
- **Some things stay complex.** Not every problem compresses to a sentence; forcing it hides what matters.
- **Simplifying can drop the caveat that matters.** Keep the one exception that would change the decision.
